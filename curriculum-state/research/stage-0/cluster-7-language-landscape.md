## 1. Key verified facts, names, dates, sources

### 1.0 The spine: why specialization happened at all
- **The 1959 survey that explains COBOL better than any story.** A 1959 US data-processing survey found the average installation had spent **US$800,000 on programming** and would spend **a further US$600,000 translating programs to run on new hardware**. The US Department of Defense operated **225 computers, had 175 more on order, and had spent over $200 million** implementing programs for them. Portability was not an aesthetic preference; it was a line item. That is the money argument behind the "common business language" — and the reason COBOL exists at all (Wikipedia, COBOL, citing Beyer 2009).
- **FORTRAN's own limits are concrete, not vague.** FORTRAN had **no character or string data type until FORTRAN 77** (only Hollerith constants before that) and **no recursion until Fortran 90** — subroutines could not call themselves, and the compiler assumed they never would. A payroll is records, files and printed reports; a physics job is numbers. FORTRAN could do the second and not the first (fortranwiki; Flang feature history).
- **The pattern the chapter teaches:** a language is a bundle of decisions about **what to make easy, what to make safe, and what to make fast** — and no language maximises all three. "General-purpose" means *not tied to one job*, not *good at every job*.
- **The flywheel, stated by the people who lived it.** Ritchie, on why C succeeded: *"the success of Unix itself was the most important factor"* — and Unix's own spread depended on C's portability. Kay, at HOPL-II, on why languages persist: *"many if not most languages are in use today not because they have any real merits but because of their existence on one or more machines, their ability to be bootstrapped."* Both are primary-source lines and both are load-bearing.
- **Two non-engineering forces worth naming once:** institutions (the DoD backed COBOL, IBM backed FORTRAN, Sun built Java, Microsoft built C#, Google built Go, Apple built Swift, JetBrains built Kotlin) and platform owners (browsers execute JavaScript; Android prefers Kotlin; iOS runs Swift).

### 1.1 Wave 1 — 1958-60: three different answers to FORTRAN's limits

- **LISP — designed for symbols, not numbers.** McCarthy's own *History of LISP* (1978): the desire for a list-processing language for AI work *"arose in the summer of 1956"* during the Dartmouth project; the **implementation began Fall 1958** at MIT; the paper is *Recursive Functions of Symbolic Expressions and Their Computation by Machine, Part I*, **CACM, April 1960**. The FORTRAN-based FLPL (Gelernter and Gerberich, IBM) had *"neither conditional expressions nor recursion"* — the two features McCarthy needed and could not get from FORTRAN. LISP invented **garbage collection** to solve the erasure problem, and by McCarthy's own 1978 line it was *"the second oldest programming language in present widespread use (after FORTRAN)."* Its ideas — recursion, GC, dynamic typing, the interactive prompt, treating code as data — are now in almost every language; the language itself moved to the AI niche. **Flag:** sources saying "LISP 1960" mean the paper; the design is 1958.
- **COBOL — a language for the people already doing the paperwork.** The record: **8 April 1959**, Mary K. Hawes (Burroughs) calls a meeting at the University of Pennsylvania with Hopper, Sammet and Gorn present, and asks the DoD to sponsor a common business language. **28-29 May 1959**, the Pentagon meeting (41 attendees, chaired by Charles A. Phillips) creates **CODASYL** and a short-range committee with a three-month deadline; Betty Holberton called that deadline *"gross optimism."* The specifications went to the Government Printing Office and appeared as **COBOL 60, April 1960**. The **first COBOL program ran on 17 August 1960** on an RCA 501, and the same program ran on an RCA and a UNIVAC machine in **December 1960** — the portability claim, demonstrated. ANSI standardised it in **1968**. Influences: Hopper's FLOW-MATIC (long names, English verbs, data separated from instructions), plus COMTRAN and FACT (Wikipedia, COBOL; Sammet, "The Early History of COBOL," HOPL-I).  **Flags (four, all real):** **(1)** the first meeting is **8 April 1959** (Hawes, Penn) and the famous Pentagon meeting is **28-29 May 1959** — Sammet's own HOPL abstract says "the May 1959 meeting in the Pentagon," and the popular April date refers to the earlier Hawes meeting; both are real and sources often cite only one. **(2)** "Who invented COBOL" — **nobody knows who coined the acronym** (Bemer claimed it; Sammet's notes say the origin is unrecorded), and **Sammet explicitly rejected the "mother of COBOL" framing**: Hopper *"was not the mother, creator, or developer of Cobol."* Hopper's real contribution is FLOW-MATIC, plus advisory influence. **(3)** "COBOL 60" is dated **April 1960** in print, but the specifications were approved **8 January 1960** after a December revision deadline — some sources say "December 1959" (draft delivered) and some "April 1960" (printed and distributed). **(4)** The design process was contentious: RCA's Howard Bromberg mailed a **tombstone engraved "COBOL"** to Phillips ($15, in 1959). Worth one line — it humanises "design by committee."
- **ALGOL — the missing link, and the reason C-style syntax looks the way it does.** A Zurich meeting in **May 1958** produced the *Preliminary Report on the International Algebraic Language* (**CACM, December 1958**) — the language originally called **IAL**, renamed ALGOL. The **ALGOL 60 report**, edited by **Peter Naur**, appeared in **CACM, May 1960**. It fixed in place **block structure, recursion, and the notation that became BNF**. It also had a fatal practical flaw: **the standard defined no input or output statements at all** — they were left to each implementation — which is a large part of why ALGOL never dominated commercially despite being the ancestor of Pascal, of C's syntax, and of nearly every `if ... { }` language since. **Flag:** the notation is called **"Backus Normal Form"** in the report; **"Backus-Naur Form"** is a later renaming that credits Naur's editing — commonly misdated as if Naur invented the notation.
- **Why all three at once is the chapter's best teaching beat:** the same three years produced a language for **symbols** (LISP), a language for **records and reports** (COBOL), and a language for **algorithms in general, defined by a standard rather than a vendor** (ALGOL). Each answers "who is this for?" — and none is a better FORTRAN, which is the whole point.

### 1.2 Wave 2 — 1967-74: the machine, the object, the question

- **Simula 67 (1967) — the actual first object-oriented language.** Ole-Johan Dahl and Kristen Nygaard at the Norwegian Computing Center: Simula I (1962-65, built for *simulations*) became **Simula 67**, a general-purpose language with **classes, objects and inheritance** (Wikipedia; ETHW milestone "Object-Oriented Programming, 1961-1967"). Every history that awards "invented OOP" to Smalltalk is wrong; Simula is the origin and Smalltalk is the populariser. Kay's own HOPL essay confirms the lineage: he read a Simula listing at Utah in 1967 and wrote *"most of my ideas from then on took their roots from Simula."*
- **C (1969-73; the conventional date is 1972).** Ritchie's own account, verbatim: *"C came into being in the years 1969-1973, in parallel with the early development of the Unix operating system; the most creative period occurred during 1972."* The chain: **BCPL** (Martin Richards, mid-1960s), then **B** (Thompson, 1969-70, untyped), then **NB, "new B"**, then C — named after Ritchie added a type system. The drivers were concrete: B was untyped, the PDP-11 was byte-addressed, floating-point was coming, and B's threaded code was too slow to replace assembly. **The Unix kernel was rewritten in C in the summer of 1973** — the act that proved a high-level language could run an operating system. K&R published in **1978**; ANSI X3J11 was formed in **summer 1983**; ANSI C arrived **December 1989**, ISO **1990**. Two lines to carry: C was *"created on a tiny machine as a tool to improve a meager programming environment,"* and *"C is hostile to automatic garbage collection"* — Ritchie's own phrase, and the exact gap Rust is built to close forty years later. **Flag:** "C, 1972" is the most-creative-year date; Ritchie's window is 1969-73, and there is no single release day.- **Smalltalk (1971 design / 1972 first real implementation).** From Kay's HOPL-II essay: the name came in **summer 1971** with the miniCOM design ("Smalltalk" as in *"children should program in..."*, and a deliberate reaction against naming systems after gods); **Smalltalk-71** was a paper design; **1972-76 is "the first real Smalltalk (-72)."** Kay wrote an interpreter in about two weeks — 4 a.m. sessions, from a hallway bet that the most powerful language in the world fits on a page of code — and **Dan Ingalls got it running** on a Nova in BASIC, then on the **Alto** (the "Interim Dynabook," built by Chuck Thacker's own three-month bet, first booted April 1973). Kay's own words for the term: *"a new design paradigm — which I called object-oriented."* **Smalltalk-76** is the first modern version (real inheritance); **Smalltalk-80** is the released one, published as the "Blue Book" in 1983. **Flags:** (i) the widely quoted *"I invented the term object-oriented, and I did not have C++ in mind"* comes from a 2003 email chain — secondary only; use the HOPL line instead. (ii) "Smalltalk 1972" is Smalltalk-72, a working prototype whose syntax looks like nothing today — do not show its code to beginners as if it were modern Smalltalk.- **SQL (1974) — a language that is deliberately not general-purpose.** Chamberlin and Boyce, *"SEQUEL: A Structured English Query Language,"* Proc. ACM SIGMOD Workshop on Data Description, Access and Control, **Ann Arbor, May 1974**; built at IBM's San Jose lab on **System R**, applying Codd's 1970 relational model. The name: **SEQUEL became SQL**, *"presumably because trademark conflicts caused IBM to switch"* — the hedge is HistoryOfInformation's, and it is the honest wording. **Raymond Boyce died in 1974**, the year the paper appeared. ANSI standardised SQL in **1986** (SQL-86), ISO in **1987**; recursive CTEs arrived with **SQL:1999**, which makes SQL Turing-complete — a fact that does not change its role, because you still cannot write a user interface, a game or an operating system in it. **Flags:** the pronunciation (classically "sequel," now usually "S-Q-L") is genuinely contested and both are defensible — say so in one line and move on; and Boyce's exact death date (18 June per one source) needs a second source before any sentence uses it.- **One line of 1970s context, not more: 1972 alone gave C, Smalltalk-72 and Prolog** (Colmerauer and Roussel, Marseille — standard dating, not re-verified in this pass): the machine-language, the objects-language and the logic-language in a single year. Also worth one line each as teaching languages with real lessons: **BASIC** (1964, Kemeny and Kurtz — designed so non-specialists could get instant answers) and **Pascal** (1970, Wirth — designed to teach structured programming).

### 1.3 Wave 3 — 1979-85: C++ and the object-oriented turn

- **C++ (1979 to 1983 to 1985).** Stroustrup's own HOPL-II section titles: *"C with Classes: 1979-1983"* and *"From C with Classes to C++: 1982-1985"*; the paper records *"the first commercial release and the printing of the book that defined C++ in October 1985."* The motive in one line: **Simula's classes without Simula's cost** — C's speed and hardware access, plus a way to organise large programs. ISO standardised C++ in **1998**. **Flag:** "C++ 1985" means the first commercial release plus *The C++ Programming Language*; the language existed as **C with Classes from 1979** and was renamed around **1983-84**. The name is a joke: C's increment operator — "one more than C" — verifiable and beginner-perfect.
- **The turn is real and worth one paragraph.** Between 1983 and 1995, object-orientation went from a PARC research idea to the default way the industry organised programs: C++ (1985), Objective-C (1981-83, Cox and Love; licensed by NeXT 1988), Python (classes from the start, 1991), Java (1995), Ruby (1995), C# (2000), and eventually **COBOL 2002** and **JavaScript's own class syntax (ES6, 2015)**. The pattern to teach: a good idea from one language gets absorbed by the others — that is how the landscape converges as well as diverges.

### 1.4 Wave 4 — 1989-95: the web, the enterprise, and readability

- **Python (started 1989, public 1991).** Conceived in the late 1980s as a successor to **ABC**; the implementation was started **December 1989** at CWI in Amsterdam as a holiday project; **version 0.9.0 was posted to alt.sources in February 1991** (the 20th is the commonly cited day); Python 1.0 followed in **January 1994**. The design value is the point: **readability above almost everything** — significant indentation, one obvious way to do a thing, a gentle learning curve — plus extensibility, which ABC lacked. Named after Monty Python, not the snake. **Flag:** "Python 1991" is the first public release; "1989" is when it was started; sources use both.
- **Java (1991, launched 1995).** The **Green Project** began at Sun in **June 1991** (Gosling, Sheridan, Naughton) to build a language for **consumer devices — TVs, set-top boxes, remote controls**; the language was first called **Oak**, renamed Java in 1994-95, and **publicly launched at SunWorld on 23 May 1995**. The technical bet: compile once to **bytecode**, run on a **virtual machine** that every kind of machine can host — *"write once, run anywhere."* Sun's own framing was a list of adjectives, and the one that mattered was **architecture-neutral**. **Flags:** (i) WORA was an engineering goal, not a guarantee — one studied source puts it well: it expressed the goal, "not the complete absence of platform differences"; (ii) the Oak-to-Java rename year varies by source (1994 vs 1995) — cite the May 1995 launch, which is solid.- **JavaScript (May to September to December 1995).** Netscape hired **Brendan Eich** in 1995 having promised him Scheme; what the browser actually needed was a small scripting language for non-programmers, on a schedule. Eich's own co-authored history (*"JavaScript: The First 20 Years,"* HOPL-IV 2020, as cited by Wikipedia) records that he **"prototyped the first Mocha implementation in ten contiguous days in May, 1995"** — roughly **6-15 May** — and demonstrated it to Netscape's engineering staff at the end of those ten days. Naming: **Mocha, then LiveScript (September 1995), then JavaScript (December 1995, a joint announcement with Sun Microsystems)**. Standardised as **ECMAScript, ECMA-262, June 1997**. The load-bearing fact for this whole chapter: **browsers execute JavaScript, and for thirty years nothing else was even a candidate** — the language's dominance is a distribution fact, not a merit judgement. **Flags:** (i) "built in ten days" is *the prototype*, not the language, and Eich's own account is the source to cite; (ii) the name is marketing — JavaScript has no technical relationship to Java, and saying so plainly is one of the chapter's best one-liners.
- **PHP (1993, released 1995).** Rasmus Lerdorf wrote the first CGI scripts in **1993** and released the package in **1995** as **"Personal Home Page Tools"** (announced 8 June 1995, widely reported); **PHP/FI** followed; **PHP 3 (1998)**, by Andi Gutmans and Zeev Suraski, is where the recursive name *"PHP: Hypertext Preprocessor"* appears. The design bet is the web's economics: **embed the code inside the page** and let it run cheaply on a shared server. **Flag:** "PHP is from 1993" and "PHP is from 1995" are both said; 1993 is when Lerdorf wrote it for himself, 1995 is when it was released.- **Ruby (1993, released 1995).** Matsumoto began designing Ruby in **1993** and released it publicly in **1995**; his own repeated framing is that he wanted *"a scripting language more powerful than Perl and more object-oriented than Python,"* and that the design goal was **programmer happiness** — the language should fit the human, not the machine. It stayed largely Japanese until **Ruby on Rails (2004)** made it global. **Flag:** the exact December 1995 release day is not verified to a primary source in this pass — say "December 1995" or just "1995," not the day.
- **Perl (1987) earns one line here** because it is the missing word in every 1990s story: the **glue language** — text processing, system administration, and the CGI scripts of the early web. Python and Ruby both define themselves against Perl, which is why the chapter needs its existence, not its depth.

### 1.5 Wave 5 — 2000-2014: platforms, scale, and safety

- **C# (2000, released 2002).** Announced at Microsoft's **PDC, 11-14 July 2000** (Microsoft's own event page lists ".NET Framework and Visual Studio.NET were announced... the C# programming language was announced and demonstrated"); lead designer **Anders Hejlsberg** (Turbo Pascal, Delphi, J++, C#); Microsoft's stated ECMA design goal was *"simple, modern, general-purpose, object-oriented"*; **C# 1.0 shipped with Visual Studio .NET in February 2002**. Context, stated carefully: Microsoft's Java-era language **J++ ended after the dispute with Sun (sued 1997, settled 2001)**, and .NET gave Microsoft a platform and a language it controlled end to end. **Flag:** announcement (July 2000) versus release (February 2002) is routinely conflated; and the widely told "# = four plus signs in C++++" name story has **no traceable primary source** — either omit it or flag it as folklore (the musical reading, "C-sharp," is what Microsoft's own materials use).- **Go (2007, public 2009).** The Go FAQ, verbatim: Griesemer, Pike and Thompson *"started sketching the goals for a new language on the white board on September 21, 2007"*; **Go became a public open source project on November 10, 2009**. The FAQ states the purpose directly: *"Go was born out of frustration with existing languages and environments for the work we were doing at Google"*; one had to choose *"either efficient compilation, efficient execution, or ease of programming"*; multiprocessors were becoming universal and most languages offered *"little help to program them efficiently and safely"*; and *"it should take at most a few seconds to build a large executable on a single computer."* Go is the language of **Docker and Kubernetes** (the FAQ names both). Go 1.0 arrived **March 2012**. The FAQ also supplies the chapter's transition line for the whole modern wave: Go *"was among the first of several new languages—Rust, Elixir, Swift, and more—that have made programming language development an active, almost mainstream field again."* **Flag:** 2007 (design), 2009 (public), 2012 (1.0) — pick the event, not the year.
- **Rust (2006, announced 2010, 1.0 in 2015).** Graydon Hoare started it as a **personal project around 2006** while at Mozilla, motivated by **memory-safety bugs** in large C/C++ codebases; Mozilla began sponsoring it in **2009** and it was **announced in 2010**; Rust 0.1 arrived **January 2012**; **Rust 1.0 shipped 15 May 2015**. The distinguishing claim: **memory safety without a garbage collector**, via compile-time ownership and borrowing — the direct answer to Ritchie's *"C is hostile to automatic garbage collection."* The evidence stack behind the motivation is strong and dated: **Microsoft engineers reported (about February 2019) that roughly 70% of Microsoft security updates over the previous 12 years addressed memory-safety vulnerabilities**, and **Chromium's own security reporting (May 2020) found about 70% of its high-severity bugs to be memory-unsafety problems, half of them use-after-free**. The proof-by-adoption: **Linux 6.1 (December 2022) merged initial Rust support** — the kernel's first new language since it was written in C. **Flags:** (i) the "broken elevator" origin anecdote is secondary-only — flag before use; (ii) five years compete for "Rust's year" (2006/2009/2010/2012/2015) — use 2010 for the announcement and 2015 for 1.0, and say which is which.- **TypeScript (2012).** Announced by Microsoft in **October 2012** (Anders Hejlsberg again); Microsoft's own framing: *"a language for application-scale JavaScript development... a superset of JavaScript that compiles to plain JavaScript"*; the stated reason was *"getting JavaScript development to scale."* TypeScript 1.0 followed in **April 2014**. It is the only language in this list whose entire reason for existing is another language's **success at scale** — JavaScript grew into hundred-thousand-line applications it was never designed for. **Flag:** the day (1 October 2012 is usually cited) rests on secondary sources in this pass.
- **Swift (2010, announced 2014).** Chris Lattner began Swift **in 2010**, essentially alone and in secret, to fix **Objective-C's safety problems**; it was **announced at WWDC on 2 June 2014** (the WWDC app itself was the first publicly released app using Swift); **Swift 1.0 shipped with Xcode 6 in September 2014**; the toolchain was open-sourced in **December 2015**. The predecessor's arc is the point: **Objective-C** (Brad Cox and Tom Love, 1981-83, ITT/Stepstone) reached Apple only because **NeXT licensed it in 1988 and Apple bought NeXT in 1996-97** — so Apple's platform spent roughly **thirty years** on a 1980s language with C's hazards, and Swift is the replacement. **Flag:** four dates (2010 start, June 2014 announcement, September 2014 release, December 2015 open source) — all real, none interchangeable.- **Kotlin (2011, 1.0 in 2016, Android-first 2019).** JetBrains announced **Project Kotlin in July 2011** (named after **Kotlin Island**, near St Petersburg); **Kotlin 1.0 arrived 15 February 2016**; **Google I/O 2017** made it officially first-class on Android; on **7 May 2019** Google declared Android *"Kotlin-first."* Adoption now: Google's own page says Kotlin is *"used by over 60% of professional Android developers"*; Wikipedia cites **over 95% of the top 1,000 Android apps on Google Play containing Kotlin code** (2024). The two reasons a beginner can hold: **null safety** — Java's most common crash, dereferencing a value that isn't there, is a compile-time error in Kotlin — and **conciseness**, with **full Java interoperability** so nothing had to be rewritten at once. **Flag:** 2011 (announced), 2016 (1.0), 2017 (Android official), 2019 (Kotlin-first) — and note the "favoured over Java" claim is specifically about **Android**, where it is documented, not about Java generally, where it is not.

### 1.6 The production present — why "old" languages are not "dead" languages

- **COBOL is still running the world's money, and the sourcing is softer than the claims.** The strongest sources: **Reuters' 2017 graphic "COBOL blues"** (widely cited for **about 43% of banking systems, 80% of in-person transactions, 95% of ATM swipes, and roughly 220 billion lines in use**); **Gartner, 1997** (**about 200 billion lines, about 80% of business programs**); **April 2020**, when **New Jersey's Governor Phil Murphy publicly asked for COBOL volunteers** for the state's unemployment system and the **IRS** patched its COBOL-based Individual Master File for CARES Act payments (CNN, CNBC, Slate, StateScoop); and **2024**, when the IRS announced a transition from COBOL to Java under its Digital First Initiative. **Flag hard:** the percentage figures originate in press and vendor estimates, not a primary census — present them as *reported estimates*, never as measured fact.
- **The teaching point is not "COBOL is old." It is that a working system's replacement cost is a feature of the system, not of the language.** Wikipedia's COBOL article documents a real 1981 threat of legal action from Travelers Insurance over COBOL-85's incompatibility with its **40-million-line** codebase, conversion costs estimated at 50 cents or more per line, and thousands of negative responses to the draft standard. That is the same economics as 1959, running in reverse: writing it was once the expensive part; replacing it is now.

## 2. The one-sentence reason each language exists — the holdable version

*For the chapter's spine, one per language. Order = first appearance. The middle column is the sentence; the right column is the constraint.*

| Language | The sentence a beginner can hold | The constraint, in one line |
|---|---|---|
| **COBOL** (1960) | A payroll is not mathematics — it is records, files and printed reports, and it should read like English to the people who do that work. | FORTRAN had no strings, no records, no report writing — and no business programmers. |
| **LISP** (1958/60) | A program should be able to work on symbols and lists as easily as FORTRAN works on numbers — including functions that call themselves. | FORTRAN could compute numbers, not manipulate symbols. |
| **ALGOL** (1958/60) | A language should be defined by a written international standard, not by one company's compiler. | Every FORTRAN was its own dialect; porting meant rewriting. |
| **C** (1972) | The people writing an operating system needed to talk to the hardware without writing assembly. | High-level languages were too far from the machine; assembly was too far from people. |
| **Smalltalk** (1972) | Software should be built from many small machines that talk to each other, not one large program. | No language let you model a system as independent, communicating parts. |
| **SQL** (1974) | Asking a question about data should not require writing a program to walk the data. | Navigating data structures by hand: slow to write, closed to non-programmers. |
| **C++** (1985) | Keep C's speed; add classes so a huge program can be organised without paying in performance. | C was fast but offered nothing for building very large systems. |
| **Python** (1991) | Code is read far more often than it is written, so make reading it the design goal. | Scripting languages existed — but were hard to read and hard to learn. |
| **Java** (1995) | One build should run on every kind of machine, because the machine should not matter. | The same program had to be recompiled for each chip and system. |
| **JavaScript** (1995) | A web page needed to react to a click, and the browser had no language. | Browsers shipped no scripting language; the deadline was weeks. |
| **PHP** (1995) | A page that asks a database and writes HTML should not need a systems programmer. | Writing web backends in C meant compiling and root access. |
| **Ruby** (1995) | Programming should be pleasant for the person doing it. | Existing scripting languages optimised for the machine, not the human. |
| **C#** (2000) | Microsoft's platform needed a language that Microsoft controlled, built for its tools. | Its Java licence had ended in a dispute with Sun. |
| **Go** (2009) | A million-line codebase, hundreds of thousands of cores, and builds that finish in seconds. | C++ and Java were too slow to compile and too hard to write concurrently. |
| **Rust** (2010) | Catch the whole class of memory bugs before the program ever runs — with no garbage collector. | The dominant systems languages produce a bug class attackers rely on. |
| **TypeScript** (2012) | JavaScript written in ten days now runs programs of a hundred thousand lines; add the checks a compiler can make. | JavaScript has no types, and big teams make big mistakes. |
| **Swift** (2014) | Apple's thirty-year-old language carried C's dangers; replace it without breaking anything. | Objective-C's C heritage: verbose, unsafe, hard to learn. |
| **Kotlin** (2011/19) | Java on Android was verbose and crashed on empty values; fix both and keep Java compatibility. | Null-pointer crashes and boilerplate were endemic on Android. |

**Presentation note for drafting:** the right-hand column is the *constraint*; the middle column is the *sentence*. The chapter's job is to make the left column memorable and the right column true. Keep the middle column visible at the chapter's end as a table the reader can return to — that table is the artefact the brief asks for: "a complete mental map of what each language is for."

## 3. The role map — which language for which job, and why

*This is the section that primes Stage 1's four chapters. Every claim must be defensible, not folklore.*

- **Frontend — JavaScript, and now TypeScript.** The reason is mechanical, not aesthetic: **the browser executes JavaScript**, so anything a user sees in a browser is written in it (with TypeScript compiling down to it). **Precision required:** the claim "JavaScript is the only language browsers run" was true for about twenty years and is now *almost* true — **WebAssembly (shipped in all major browsers by November 2017)** is a second, newer format browsers execute, but MDN defines it as *"designed to complement and run alongside JavaScript,"* and it is a **compilation target** for C/C++, C# and Rust rather than a language people write by hand. State it as: *browsers run JavaScript directly; WebAssembly is a newer second format for compiled code, which works with JavaScript rather than replacing it.*
- **Backend — general-purpose languages, chosen by culture and ecosystem rather than capability.** **Python** (readability, huge ecosystem, now the AI default — Stack Overflow's 2025 survey records its largest jump, and **GitHub's Octoverse 2024 has Python overtaking JavaScript as the most-used language on GitHub**), **Java** (decades of enterprise systems on the JVM), **C#** (Microsoft and .NET shops), **Go** (cloud infrastructure: Docker and Kubernetes), **JavaScript via Node.js** — reuse the ledger's canonical wording exactly: *"a runtime that lets JavaScript, a language born for browsers, run outside one"* — **PHP** (the web's embedded server language, still a very large share of deployed sites), **Ruby** (Ruby on Rails). **C++ appears here too**, for latency-critical servers — which is exactly why "backend" must be taught as *a job, not a language*: the same job is done in five languages by five companies.
- **Systems — C, C++, Rust.** Programs that manage memory, run other programs, or must not be slow: operating systems, databases, browsers, device drivers, embedded chips. The arc to teach: **C (1972)** gave *control*; **C++ (1985)** gave control plus structure; **Rust (2010)** gives control plus compile-time memory safety. The evidence stack from section 1.5 explains *why* the third one exists, and **Linux 6.1 (December 2022)** is the proof-by-adoption.- **Data and query — SQL.** Not a general-purpose language: it *describes the answer you want*, and the database decides how to find it (the word to gloss at first use: **declarative**, as against every language above, which is **imperative** — you write the steps). One-line calibration for the reader: *SQL is the language you will meet again in the databases chapter and in Stage 2, and you will never write a user interface in it.* The database *history* (Codd 1970, System R, Ingres, NoSQL) belongs to cluster 6 and Stage 1's s1-05; this chapter stops at the category.
- **Mobile — Swift and Kotlin, the two platform languages.** The only row where a *company* decides the answer: **iOS and macOS to Swift** (Apple's own replacement for Objective-C), **Android to Kotlin** (Google's declared preference — "Kotlin-first," May 2019). The honest caveat for a beginner: cross-platform tools exist (Flutter, React Native), but the two platform languages are the defaults, and both exist because the platforms' previous languages aged badly — one thirty years old, the other with pervasive null crashes.
- **The category the chapter should name and drop: glue and shell** (Bash, PowerShell) — small languages for orchestrating other programs, owned by the Terminals chapter and indispensable to this book's own subject, since an agent drives a shell. And **markup is not programming**: HTML and CSS describe structure and appearance; they do not decide anything. One line, no more — the reader meets both in Stage 1's frontend chapter.
- **Two honesty rules for the whole map.** **(i) "General-purpose" is a claim about scope, not quality** — every general-purpose language here is Turing-complete, so all of them *can* do almost any job; the differences are what they make easy, safe and fast, and what ecosystem surrounds them. **(ii) Popularity measurements disagree by construction:** Stack Overflow's 2025 survey puts **JavaScript first (about 66%, with Python rising fastest)**; GitHub's Octoverse 2024 puts **Python first on GitHub**; TIOBE measures search-engine traffic, not work. Teach the reader that "the most popular language" is a question about *which measurement*, not a fact.

## 4. How strong teaching sources sequence and group this material

- **The scholarly record sequences by language, in first person: HOPL.** ACM SIGPLAN's History of Programming Languages conferences (1978, 1993, 2007, 2021) are the authoritative source behind most secondary accounts — the 1978 proceedings record *"a baker's dozen of the languages that set the tone of most of today's programming,"* told by the people who made them. This is where the primary quotations in section 1 come from (Ritchie on C, Kay on Smalltalk, Stroustrup on C++, McCarthy on LISP). **Not a beginner source — it is the authority this chapter's claims rest on.**
- **Jean Sammet's *Programming Languages: History and Fundamentals* (1969)** — described by HOPL-IV's own material as *"an encyclopedic survey of programming languages as of 1969."* The first systematic attempt to do what this chapter does; organised by language family, chronologically. Its limitation is the chapter's opening: it is an encyclopaedia, not a teaching text.
- **The canonical university chapter is Sebesta's "Evolution of the Major Programming Languages"** (*Concepts of Programming Languages*, now in its 12th edition). Its structure, verified from the publisher's own table of contents: **strictly chronological, one language per section** — 2.1 Zuse's Plankalkul, 2.3 the IBM 704 and Fortran, 2.4 Functional Programming: Lisp, 2.5 ALGOL 60, 2.6 COBOL, 2.7 BASIC, 2.8 PL/I — plus "History Notes" and **interviews with the designers** (Stroustrup, Alan Cooper) beside the language each designed. **What to borrow: the reasoning** — each section names the problem the language was built to solve and the language it reacted against. **What not to borrow:** its order and completeness (it is written for CS majors who may implement languages, not for a beginner building a mental map), and none of its text.
- **Beginner video source: Crash Course Computer Science #11, "The First Programming Languages."** It opens on the problem (programming at the hardware level, with plugboards) and ends with FORTRAN opening "a golden age of computing languages" — **problem first, date second.** Useful for register and pacing; it stops at the boundary cluster 1 also stops at.- **CS-education sources teach one language at a time, in the order the student will use them — this is the key finding.** **CS50x** runs Scratch, then C, then Python, then SQL, then HTML/CSS/JavaScript, then Flask: five languages, none surveyed, each introduced as the tool for the next job. **The Odin Project** organises two complete paths (Foundations, then either Full Stack JavaScript or Full Stack Ruby on Rails): the language *is* the path. **freeCodeCamp** and MDN's learning paths do the same. **No beginner-facing source found teaches the landscape itself** — the closest is Sebesta, for majors. This chapter is therefore filling a real, verified gap, exactly as clusters 1 and 5 found for their own topics.
- **The "generations of programming languages" framing (1GL machine code, 2GL assembly, 3GL high-level, 4GL declarative, 5GL AI/logic) is widely taught in schools and should be used with a caveat or not at all.** It is readable but analytically thin at the top end: "4GL" was largely a marketing category and "5GL" is loose enough to mean anything. If used, present it as a *label system people invented afterwards*, not as a real family tree — which is itself a good lesson for a book whose thesis is "verify before you trust."
- **The shared move across every strong source, restated from cluster 1 because it binds this chapter too:** each new language is introduced because **the existing ones failed at something specific** — problem first, solution second, never date first.

## 5. Story and analogy hooks

1. **The same three years, two opposite bets (1958-60).** COBOL says a program should read like the paperwork it replaces (*MOVE x TO y*); LISP says a program should be mathematics and its data should be lists. Both are reactions to FORTRAN; neither is like the other. Show them side by side — this is the chapter's opening image for "specialization."
2. **The tombstone (1959).** Frustrated by the committee, RCA's Howard Bromberg mailed a $15 tombstone engraved "COBOL" to the DoD sponsor. A language designed by committee, and its designers knew it.
3. **The bet and the 4 a.m. interpreter (1972).** Kay claimed the most powerful language in the world fits on one page; he wrote Smalltalk-72's interpreter in about two weeks before anyone else arrived at PARC, and Dan Ingalls had it running on real hardware within days (in BASIC, on a Nova). *"The best way to predict the future is to invent it"* is Kay's own line from the same essay.
4. **Written on a whiteboard on 21 September 2007, released two years later.** Go's origin, from its own FAQ, and the honest motivation: three things you could never have at once — fast compilation, fast execution, ease of writing.
5. **The ten days.** JavaScript's prototype was written in about ten days in May 1995, by one person, for one browser, to a deadline — and it became the only language every browser executes. The lesson is not "fast is good" but "distribution beats design."6. **Languages do not die; they retire to a job.** FORTRAN runs physics; COBOL runs banks and payroll; C runs operating systems; SQL runs every question asked of a relational database. Two kinds of survival, one sentence each: **COBOL's programs outlived the language's reputation**, and **LISP's ideas outlived the language** — garbage collection, recursion, dynamic typing and the interactive prompt are now in nearly everything.
7. **The same rule in two languages.** A worked illustration for the chapter: the thirty-seat cap — the intro sequence's own club sign-up page — expressed once in Python and once in JavaScript, doing the *same* job in a backend and in a browser. Same rule, two homes: the cleanest way to make "which language for which job" concrete without teaching syntax.
8. **The number behind Rust.** About 70% of Microsoft's security fixes over twelve years, and about 70% of Chromium's serious bugs, are memory mistakes in C and C++. One statistic converts "Rust is trendy" into "Rust is an answer to a measured problem."
9. **The platform decides.** iOS runs Swift because Apple says so; Android prefers Kotlin because Google says so. The one part of the map where the answer is not technical.
10. **The mismatch to laugh at, then explain:** JavaScript is to Java as *car* is to *carpet* — the name was a marketing arrangement with Sun, signed in December 1995.

## 6. Structural recommendation — lessons, placement, and the reasoning

### 6.1 The three options, tested honestly

- **Option A — extend Chapter 1's "the language ladder" Lesson: NO.** Three independent reasons. **(1) A window violation, on an owner-resolved rule:** Chapter 1's ladder ends at FORTRAN, April 1957, and the stage's ordering rule is chronological; this material runs to 2014. **(2) It would teach two different shapes in one Lesson:** the ladder is *vertical* (closer to the machine, then closer to people — a proof that a high-level language is possible); this material is *horizontal* (which language for which job — a map). Fusing them trips the "too long or mixed" trigger the finer-granularity rule watches for. **(3) Comprehension order:** a reader cannot understand why Java (1995) or Go (2009) exist before meeting the server, the browser and the network — which arrive in later chapters. **What Chapter 1's Lesson does owe:** a one-sentence handoff, in the project's existing "hand off, don't land" style — FORTRAN was the proof; *who is this for* is a later question — so the ladder closes with a pointer instead of a full stop.
- **Option B — fold into cluster 6's new software-architecture chapter: NO, with two written boundary lines.** The architecture chapter's subject is **system shape** (client-server, three-tier/SOA/microservices, containers, auth, database-model history); this chapter's subject is **notation and ecosystem**. Merging them would produce a two-topic chapter — already a length risk for cluster 6's material — and would cost the map its own job: priming *three* Stage 1 chapters at once (core programming, frontend, backend). The two do share real ground, so the boundary must be written down: **cluster 6 owns SQL-as-database-history and the shape of the client-server split; this chapter owns SQL-as-language-category and the language map.** The database-model history is not restated here, and the language typology is not restated there.
- **Option C — its own distinct chapter: YES.** That is the recommendation, with placement and Lesson structure below.

### 6.2 Placement: its own chapter, **late in Phase B — immediately before the SDE destination chapter**

Reasoning, in order of weight:

1. **The material's own shape forces a late slot.** The chapter cannot sit where its earliest facts belong (1958-60), because its job is a map that runs to 2014. Every candidate early slot — after Chapter 1, inside Chapter 2's 1958-74 window, after the Web chapter — would force either a false chronology (a year-2000-capped chapter containing Kotlin) or a chapter that ends before the languages the reader will actually meet in Stage 1.
2. **Stage 0 has already solved this exact problem twice, both times the same way.** The AI chapter opens by naming its own out-of-order placement with a dated anchor, and Phase B is explicitly ordered *"by where each thread ends rather than where it began, closing on the present."* The language thread is the same kind of thread: it starts in 1958 and never stops. The chapter opens by saying so — one dated sentence, the established pattern, no apology.
3. **Recency is the point.** The chapter's stated job is to prime Stage 1's core-programming, frontend, backend and databases chapters. Immediately before the destination chapter, it is the last content the reader meets before Stage 1 begins — and it can point forward by name at the two languages this book goes deep on (**CS50P is Python; CS50W is JavaScript/Django/SQL**), which is exactly the priming the brief asks for.
4. **It hands off to the destination chapter cleanly.** SDE's claim is that a specification must be verifiable against code you can read and judge — and reading and judging code *requires knowing what the language is for*: a C program's risks are not a Python program's risks, and a SQL query has no memory to manage at all. The language chapter is the last piece of equipment the reader picks up before the destination chapter tells them what to do with it. That is an earned ordering, not an arbitrary one.
5. **It must not sit *after* the destination chapter.** SDE plus Reading and Understanding Literacy is the stage's finale, and the owner's own framing of Stage 0 is to make the reader ready to understand the SDE. A tools chapter after the finale would read as an appendix.

**The alternative honestly stated, and why it loses.** If strict chronology is judged paramount, the consistent alternative is to **split** the material — the first wave (LISP/COBOL/ALGOL, 1958-60) into Chapter 2's 1958-74 window as a fifth thread, and the modern map as a late chapter. It loses because Chapter 2 is already a four-thread chapter with its own concurrency structure, and because a map split across six intervening chapters is not a map: the reader would meet the first half as history and the second half as reference, and a chapter whose entire purpose is to leave the reader with one complete mental map would stop being one document. Flag it to the owner as the consistent-if-not-preferred fallback, not as the recommendation.

### 6.3 Lesson breakdown — two Lessons, three as the available seam

**The chapter (working titles: "A Language for Every Job" / "Why There Is More Than One Language" / "The Language Map").** *The title must promise the map, not the timeline — the timeline is Stage 0's spine everywhere else; this chapter's distinction is that its organising principle is purpose.*

- **Lesson 1 — Why one language was never enough (1958-74).** The three first answers to FORTRAN (COBOL, LISP, ALGOL, taught as three different *constraints*), then the 1970s generation: C (the machine), SQL (the question), Smalltalk and Simula (the objects). Closes on the realisation that by 1974 there were already several languages and each existed for a job — the landscape is not a mistake to be tidied up later. Carries the LISP/AI and C/Unix callbacks, and the tombstone and 4 a.m.-interpreter hooks.
- **Lesson 2 — The map you will actually use (1991-2014).** Organized **by role, not by date**: frontend (JavaScript/TypeScript), backend (Python, Java, C#, Go, Node, PHP, Ruby), systems (C/C++/Rust), data (SQL), mobile (Swift, Kotlin). Each language gets its one sentence from section 2 plus its constraint; the 1990s and 2000s appear as *when and why they appeared*, not as a second timeline. Closes on the **role table** the reader can return to, and the forward pointer into Stage 1's four chapters.
- **The available seam, if Lesson 2 runs long (the finer-granularity rule applies):** split it into **(2a) the web's languages (1991-2000: Python, Java, JavaScript, PHP, Ruby — the 1990s explosion, with the browser's monopoly as its centrepiece)** and **(2b) the platform and scale languages (2000-2014: C#, Go, Rust, TypeScript, Swift, Kotlin)**. Take the seam if the draft crosses roughly 2,500-3,000 words for Lesson 2; the two halves answer different questions ("why did the web produce languages?" versus "why did companies start building languages again?") and are not one continuous read.

### 6.4 Boundary guards this chapter must respect

- **No syntax teaching.** Not one line of code in languages the reader will meet in Stage 1. This is the *map*; Stage 1 is the *territory*. Even the two-language club-page illustration is about *roles*, not a tutorial.
- **No AI depth for LISP, no Unix depth for C, no database history for SQL** — one paragraph each, each with an explicit callback to the chapter that owns it.
- **No invented popularity claims.** "Most popular language" must always name its measurement (Stack Overflow survey, GitHub Octoverse, TIOBE) or not be said.
- **No unglossed vocabulary.** New terms this chapter would introduce that are not yet in `concept-ledger.yaml` include: **object / class / object-oriented**, **virtual machine**, **garbage collection**, **declarative versus imperative**, **type system / static typing**, **concurrency**, and **platform language**. Each needs either a prior teaching beat or an inline gloss at first use (the per-chapter rule admits no exceptions), and each should be added to the ledger with canonical wording at drafting time. Existing canonical wording to reuse verbatim rather than re-invent: **high-level language**, **compiler**, **interpreter**, **abstraction**, **framework**, **Node.js**.
- **The worked-object question.** If the chapter needs one, the candidate is the club page's thirty-seat rule expressed in two languages (Python and JavaScript) — but this is the example ledger's own shared object, so check `example-ledger.yaml` before claiming it, and do not introduce a second teaching object without a ledger entry: the Stage 1 precedent is explicit that no chapter introduced a second object.

## 8. Genuinely disputed, or not verified in this pass

1. **COBOL's first meeting.** 8 April 1959 (Hawes, University of Pennsylvania) versus "the May 1959 meeting in the Pentagon" (Sammet's own HOPL abstract). Both meetings happened; sources cite one or the other. The Pentagon meeting is 28-29 May 1959.
2. **COBOL's production percentages.** The 43% / 80% / 95% / 220-billion-lines figures all trace to press and vendor estimates (Reuters 2017, Gartner 1997), not a measured census. Report as estimates.
3. **"COBOL 60's" date.** Specifications approved 8 January 1960; printed and distributed April 1960; some sources say December 1959. Say "COBOL 60, April 1960" and add the approval date only if precision matters.
4. **Who "invented COBOL," and Grace Hopper's role.** The acronym's coiner is unrecorded (Bemer claimed it). Sammet rejected the "mother of COBOL" framing outright. Give Hopper FLOW-MATIC and advisory credit, not authorship.
5. **C's year.** 1972 (conventional) versus 1969-73 (Ritchie's own window). Use 1972 for the chronology with the window stated in one clause.
6. **C++'s year.** 1985 (first commercial release plus the book), 1979 (C with Classes), 1983 (rename), 1998 (ISO). Same treatment.
7. **Rust's year.** Five candidates (2006 start, 2009 Mozilla, 2010 announced, 2012 first release, 2015 1.0). Wherever the chapter places it, say which event the date marks.
8. **Go's year.** 2007 (whiteboard), 2009 (public), 2012 (1.0). Same.
9. **Kotlin's year.** 2011 (announced), 2016 (1.0), 2017 (Android official), 2019 (Kotlin-first). Same.
10. **Swift's date.** June 2014 announcement, September 2014 release, December 2015 open source; development from 2010. Same.
11. **JavaScript's "ten days."** True of the May 1995 prototype only (Eich's own account: roughly 6-15 May). It must not be used to describe the finished language.
12. **PHP's date.** 1993 (Lerdorf writes it) versus 1995 (public release). The 8 June 1995 announcement date is widely reported but rests on weak sources — verify before printing the day.
13. **Ruby's exact first-release day.** "December 1995" is safe; the day is not verified here.
14. **Java's rename year.** 1994 versus 1995 in different sources; the 23 May 1995 public launch is solid.
15. **"Write once, run anywhere."** Aspirational, not a guarantee; sources themselves hedge. Teach the goal, not the slogan.
16. **C#'s "#" origin.** The "four plus signs" story has no traceable primary source; the musical reading is what Microsoft's materials use. Omit or flag as folklore.
17. **Boyce's death date.** 1974 is certain; the day (18 June?) is single-sourced. Do not print the day without a second source.
18. **SQL's pronunciation.** "Sequel" versus "S-Q-L" — genuinely contested; both defensible; say so in one line.
19. **SQL's rename reason.** "Presumably trademark" is the sources' own hedge — keep it.
20. **Smalltalk's OOP claim.** Simula 67 is the first object-oriented language; Smalltalk popularised the paradigm. The Kay "I did not have C++ in mind" quote is secondary-only; use the HOPL line instead.
21. **The OOP term's coinage year.** Kay's HOPL essay is the primary source for "which I called object-oriented"; the often-cited 1967 date is not corroborated there.
22. **Any "X is replacing Y" sentence.** Rust replacing C++, Swift replacing Objective-C, Kotlin replacing Java each need a scope — the kernel, iOS development, Android respectively — not a general claim.
23. **Unverified in this pass and therefore not asserted:** Prolog's 1972 dating (standard, not re-checked); the exact Stack Overflow question counts behind the 2025 survey; the "Rust was born from a broken elevator" anecdote (secondary-only); the 1 October 2012 TypeScript announcement day.

## 9. What every studied source missed — and what this chapter therefore owns

1. **No beginner-facing source teaches the language landscape as a map.** Teach-one-language curricula (CS50, Odin, freeCodeCamp) teach roles by osmosis; textbooks survey chronologically, for majors; encyclopaedias list. The Stage 0 chapter is the synthesis — and it inherits the stage's beginner-only register.
2. **Nobody connects the paradigm shift back to the constraint.** "Object-oriented" is taught as a *thing languages have*, not as an answer to "how do you build a system too big to hold in one head?" (Simula) and "how do you stop state from being everywhere?" (Kay). Two chapters' worth of depth in section 1.2 compresses to two sentences.
3. **Nobody teaches that COBOL and LISP both "won," in opposite ways.** COBOL's *programs* outlived its reputation; LISP's *ideas* outlived the language. That pair is the chapter's best closing beat, and no studied source says it.
4. **No source explains JavaScript's dominance as distribution, not design** — and therefore no source prepares a beginner for the practical consequence: if it runs in a browser, this is the language, for better or worse.
5. **No source puts the memory-safety statistics and Rust in one beginner-legible paragraph.** The statistics exist (Microsoft, Chromium), the language exists, and the connection is left implicit in everything studied.
6. **No source teaches "general-purpose is a scope claim, not a quality claim"** — and its absence is exactly why beginners ask "which language is best?" The map answers a better question: which language for which job, and why.
