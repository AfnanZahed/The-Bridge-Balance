# Canon — how everything is named

*Set by the owner on 20 September 2026, after "Stage 0 — Orientation Through
Computing History" survived four days past being rejected for the word
"Orientation", and kept appearing in chapter prose and in the site's own chapter
header.*

This file governs the name of every **stage, chapter, lesson, part, section
heading and reference page** in this book. Three rules, all of which must hold.

*Which levels exist, and what kind of name each level gets (what is learned, never the reader's level), is decided in `course-structure.md` (CS-3). This file decides how a name is written.*

## Rule 1 — The name explains itself

**A reader who has not read the content must understand what it is about from
the name alone.** They should not have to read the material to work out what the
title meant.

- Plain words a beginner already owns. If a reader needs a dictionary for the
  title, the title has failed before the page starts.
- The name says what the thing *is about*, not how the project thinks about it
  internally.
- Clever, abstract or atmospheric titles are wrong here, however good they look.
  "Yes, No, and Nothing In Between" tells a reader nothing; "How a Computer
  Says Yes and No" tells them everything.

**Rejected by the owner, and not to be reintroduced:** *Orientation*.

**Test it like this.** Show the title alone to someone who has never programmed.
Ask what they think it covers. If they cannot answer, or answer wrongly, rename
it.

## Rule 2 — It reads like an international, professional course

**The naming and organisation follow international standards and
ultra-professional wording**, the way a recognised course names its parts.

The owner's example: Harvard CS50's *Introduction to Programming with Python* —
that register, that clarity, that seriousness. Not a copy of anyone's titles;
the standard they are written to.

- Sentence-clear and specific: "Introduction to Databases", not "Data, Deeply".
- No marketing voice, no hype, no exclamation in a title.
- Consistent shape across siblings — chapters within a stage should read as a
  set, not as a pile of one-off ideas.
- British spelling (`audience.md`), and the same capitalisation style across
  every level.


## Rule 3 - The name carries the why, and the why is the reader's destination

*Added by the owner on 20 September 2026:* "the headings, names and titles of
stages, chapters, lessons and parts (if any) must give the meaning of WHAT they
actually are, but for the beginners, the names MUST also give them the sense of
WHY... in first 3 seconds, they don't have the context and the WHY in their
subconscious minds."

*Corrected by the owner the same day, because the first version of this rule
produced the wrong kind of why:* "that WHY are going to be changed 100% on every
topic... ofcourse the curriculum desginer is ME (afnan zahed) and I will tell
them in my own natural language that WHY I put this content and then the name
will be engineered propelry with the WHAT and WHY. that is the meaning of 'WHY',
not the WHY of the invention or tool itself."

Rule 1 makes a name **understandable**. It does not make it **wanted**. A name
answers two questions at once:

1. **What is this?** - Rule 1.
2. **Why am I, someone learning to specify, bound, verify and own AI-built
   work, reading it?** - this rule.

### The why is the curriculum's, never the subject's

This is the distinction the first draft of this rule got wrong, and it is the
whole rule.

**Not this.** The why of the invention, the tool or the idea *in isolation* -
what it did for the world, why it was impressive, what it made possible in
general. *"The Switch That Made Computers Small Enough to Own"* is a true and
interesting fact about the transistor. It is **not a why**. It tells the reader
nothing about why this page is in front of *them*.

**This.** Why the **curriculum designer put this topic in this book**, and what
this step gives the reader on the way to the destination the book exists for
(`canon/thesis.md`). A reader is here to become someone who can specify, bound,
verify and own what an AI agent produces. Every name connects its subject to
that.

**The why changes completely for every topic.** There is no template and no
reusable phrase. The same subject in a different curriculum would carry a
different why, and the name would come out different.

### The three-second test

Show the name alone, with nothing around it, for three seconds. If the reader
cannot say *what it is* **and** *what they get from it*, the name has failed.

This is not a stylistic preference. A beginner arrives with **no context at
all** - no previous chapter in their head, nothing that told them why this
subject exists or where it leads. The name is the only thing carrying that, and
it is doing its work in a sidebar, in a search result, on a contents page,
before any opening paragraph exists to rescue it. By the time they reach the
first sentence, the decision to read has already been made.

`corrections.md` §7 fixed the *opening* so it says why the chapter matters.
This rule fixes the thing a reader meets **before** the opening.

### The why is an input. The writer does not invent it

**The curriculum designer is the owner.** They decide what goes into this book
and why it is there, and they state it in their own natural language. The
writer's job is to take that sentence and **engineer a name from it** - the WHAT
plus that WHY, under Rules 1 and 2. The writer chooses the wording. It does not
choose the why.

**If the owner's why for a topic has not been supplied, stop and ask for it.**
Do not infer it, do not reconstruct it from the stage table, and do not write a
plausible one. A name built on a guessed why fails in the worst possible way: it
reads perfectly well and points the reader at the wrong destination. This is the
same hard stop `canon/research-and-comparison.md` places on the owner's
comparative study - a required input, not a nice-to-have.

### How a name carries the curriculum's why

The book's destination is `thesis.md`'s four verbs. A name usually connects to
one of them. **The owner's own sentence outranks this table** - it is a starting
shape for engineering their words into a title, not a menu to pick from.

| The move | What the topic is connected to |
|---|---|
| What the reader will be able to **judge** because of this | verify |
| What they will be able to **ask for precisely** because of this | specify |
| Which limit they will be able to **set** because of this | bound |
| What they will be able to **take responsibility for** because of this | own |

### What is not a why

- **A fact about the subject.** "The Switch That Made Computers Small Enough to
  Own" - the subject's own significance, not the reader's reason.
- **Excitement.** "The Amazing World of Binary" promises nothing, and Rule 2
  rejects it anyway.
- **Urgency.** "You Must Understand This First" is a demand, not a reason.
- **A claim that the subject is important.** "The Foundation of All Computing" -
  a beginner has no way to judge that, and no reason yet to believe it.

A why is **what this step gives the reader on the way to the destination**.

### Scope, and where it stops

Every **stage, chapter, lesson, part and section heading**. It stops at
reference pages - *Glossary*, *Changelog*, *FAQ*, *Code of Conduct* - which are
looked up deliberately rather than chosen from a list. There Rule 1 alone
governs, and a why would be noise.

### The why is usually short

Long titles are not the goal; **earned** titles are. One clause is normally
enough: "How a Machine Holds a Number" becomes "How a Machine Holds a Number,
and Why It Only Needs Two." If a name needs a whole sentence to earn its why,
the section under it is probably two sections.

## The three rules together

They pull against each other on purpose. Rule 1 pushes toward plain and
obvious, Rule 2 toward formal and credible, Rule 3 toward a reason to care. A
good name is all three. Settle them in the order **1 -> 3 -> 2**: make it clear,
give it a why, then check it still reads like a serious course.

| Weak | Which rule it fails | Better |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation Through Computing History | R1 - "Orientation" is not a beginner's word | Introduction to Computing: From Switches to AI Agents |
| Yes, No, and Nothing In Between | R1 - says nothing about the subject | How a Computer Says Yes and No |
| Programming Is Born | R1 - atmospheric; a reader cannot tell what they will learn | How People First Told a Computer What to Do |
| Data, Deeply | R2 - marketing voice | Introduction to Databases |
| Short Words Instead of Numbers | R3 - true, and no reason to care | Needs the owner's why for this topic first |
| Foundations | R1 and R3 - an internal label, and an empty promise | R1 is fixable now ("How a Machine Holds a Number"); R3 needs the owner's why |
| The Switch That Made Computers Small Enough to Own | **R3, the wrong kind of why** - a fact about the transistor, not a reason this reader is reading about it | Needs the owner's why for this topic first |
| The Amazing World of Binary | R2 and R3 - excitement standing in for a reason | Needs the owner's why for this topic first |

The last three rows are deliberately unresolved. **A name cannot be finished
without the owner's why**, and filling those cells with a confident guess is the
precise failure this rule exists to stop.

## Supporting conventions

- **A date range after a Stage 0 chapter title is kept** — "1 — Foundations ·
  1679–1948". It signals that this stage is read chronologically, which is the
  one thing about Stage 0 a reader benefits from seeing up front. It does not
  license dates inside the prose (`corrections.md` §4).
- **Never put an internal label in a name.** No *station*, *spine*, *register*,
  *shape*, *front door*, *invariant*, *Reading and Understanding Literacy*.
  `corrections.md` §9.
- **A name is not a promise of a count.** No "Chapter 7 of 20" in a title.

## When a name changes

1. Check both rules, and write down the one-line reason the old name fails.
2. **Propose the new name to the owner before applying it.** A name appears in
   the prose, the sidebar, the chapter header, the ledgers and every cross-link;
   renaming is cheap to propose and expensive to undo.
3. Once agreed, change it **everywhere in the same pass** — the file, the
   frontmatter, `prerequisite-graph.yaml`, `sidebars.ts`, any stage table in
   `canon/thesis.md`, and every chapter that names it in prose.
